## Corrected — could not be verified

> **Superseded in part.** This is the original audit, kept as written. Hagedorn (1921)
> has since been added to the corpus and every item below was re-run against it; see
> **RESOLVED** further down for what was restored and what was not.

Five claims were presented as quotations or hard figures and could not be sourced.
All have been rewritten rather than removed, because the substance is sound; what
could not be established was the exact wording or the number.

1. **"I never would have been President if it had not been for my experiences in
   North Dakota."** Not in the Autobiography. Reaches us through secondary sources,
   in at least two different wordings — one with "here in North Dakota," one without
   — and one biographer hedges it as something Roosevelt "speculated." Now reported
   as recollection, not presented as a verbatim quotation. **This is the Library's own
   founding line and is worth a definitive source hunt before it is used in signage
   or fundraising copy.**
2. **"He had grit. I liked him from the start."** (Joe Ferris) — no match. Rewritten
   as substance without quotation marks.
3. **"I never saw anyone so enthused in my life… beat the dead buffalo with his hat."**
   (Joe Ferris) — no match. Now attributed to Ferris's later recollection, unquoted.
4. **"we were far too remote from the county seat for the law to concern itself with us."**
   Could not confirm the continuation of the Autobiography sentence. Removed.
5. **Numbers:** the 300,000-head buffalo herd, "seven miles south of Medora," and the
   ~$80,000 total loss could not be corroborated. All three softened to what the
   sources support.

Also corrected: the claim that "Old Four Eyes" is a later coinage. That is plausible
and widely repeated but not sourceable; the explanation now says only what is attested.

## Why four quotations failed

All four trace to Hermann Hagedorn's *Roosevelt in the Bad Lands* (1921), which at the
time of the audit was **not in the corpus** — the Hagedorn title held then was
*A Theodore Roosevelt Round-Up*. They are probably genuine. They were not verifiable
from what we held, and the standard is that unverifiable wording does not go inside
quotation marks.

### 1. The founding line — NOT resolved

`"I never would have been President if it had not been for my experiences in North
Dakota."` A whitespace-normalised search of the whole Hagedorn text returns **zero**
hits for *never would have been president*, *would never have been president*, *been
president if*, *experiences in North Dakota*, and *in North Dakota* in any form. It is
not there.

What Hagedorn does print, as the epigraph facing his first chapter, is Roosevelt's
**Sioux Falls speech of September 3, 1910** — a dated, attributable, verbatim statement
to the same effect:

> My friends, I never can sufficiently express the obligations I am under to the
> territory of Dakota, for it was here that I lived a number of years in a ranch house
> in the cattle country, and I regard my experience during those years, when I lived
> and worked with my own fellow ranchmen on what was then the frontier, as the most
> important educational asset of all my life.

The famous line itself still reaches us only through secondary works, and they do not
agree. Gessner and O'Keefe print "here in North Dakota"; Dalton prints it both ways and
her endnote traces it no further back than Elwyn B. Robinson's *History of North Dakota*
(1966); Michael L. Collins hedges it as something Roosevelt "speculated"; Clay Jenkinson
devotes a chapter to it, calls it "his statement of 1910," and cites no manuscript.

**Recommendation for signage and fundraising copy: use the Sioux Falls sentence.** It
says the same thing, it is dated, it is Roosevelt's own, and it will survive a reporter.

### 2. "He had grit. I liked him from the start." — not Hagedorn's wording

No hits. What Hagedorn actually prints, of Joe Ferris on the buffalo hunt:

> "He could stand an awful lot of hard knocks," Joe explained later, "and he was always
> cheerful. You just couldn't knock him out of sorts."

The quiz now quotes that instead.

### 3. The buffalo — quotation restored, hat detail dropped

Hagedorn, p. 45, on the moment the bull went down:

> Roosevelt, with all his intellectual maturity, was a good deal of a boy, and the
> Indian war-dance he executed around the prostrate buffalo left nothing in the way of
> delight unexpressed. Joe watched the performance open-mouthed.
> "I never saw any one so enthused in my life," he said in after days, "and, by golly,
> I was enthused myself for more reasons than one."

Note **"any one," two words**, as printed. The quotation is restored verbatim.

The **hat is not in Hagedorn and is not anywhere in the corpus.** Auchincloss has Ferris
saying Roosevelt "broke into a wild facsimile of an Indian war dance"; R. L. Wilson has
him "improvised an Indian war dance." Nobody has him beating the carcass with his hat.
The answer option said he did; it has been rewritten to the war dance. This one was
worse than an unsourced quotation — it was an invented detail inside a correct answer.

### 5. The numbers

Two of the three are now verbatim in Hagedorn and have been restored:

> Three hundred thousand buffalo, driven north from the more settled plains of western
> Nebraska, and huddled in a territory covering not more than a hundred and fifty
> square miles, perished like cattle in a stockyard, almost overnight.

> Roosevelt's first weeks at the Maltese Cross proved one thing to him beyond debate;
> that was, that the cabin seven miles south of Medora was not the best place in the
> world to do literary work.

The ~$80,000 loss is **not** recoverable from this scan. Hagedorn gives the pieces —
$10,000 in the Teschmaker and Debillier company, the $14,000 check handed to Sylvane on
a log at Cannonball Creek, a further $26,000, the herd valued at $60,000 in the
September 1886 contract, and Roosevelt to Corinne after the die-up: "I wish I was sure
I would lose no more than half the money I invested out here" — but the footnote at
p. 458 says *"See Appendix for a statement of Roosevelt's cattle investment,"* and the
appendix is not in the OCR. The figure stays out until someone reads a physical copy.

### Bonus: "Old Four Eyes"

The audit had softened this as plausible but unsourceable. Hagedorn sources it. The
cowpunchers called him "Four Eyes" from the start because his large round glasses made
him look "very much like a curiously nervous and emphatic owl." Then, after the
Mingusville barroom:

> "Four-eyes" became, overnight, "Old Four Eyes," which was another matter.

The Q2 explanation now says so.

## Two notes

**Grand Canyon.** Sources in the corpus disagree: two say it became a national park
in 1919, one says 1917. The quiz says 1919, which is correct — the Grand Canyon
National Park Act was signed in February 1919. Flagged because the 1917 error is in
a book on our own shelf.

**T.R.'s blind eye.** Sources give the boxing injury as 1904 and as 1908. The quiz
deliberately says only "while he was president" and names no year. Leave it that way.

**Single-sourced — not corpus-verifiable.** The Legacy quiz states the Fraser
equestrian statue was removed in 2022 and sent on long-term loan to the Library.
That is institutional knowledge, not something the book corpus can confirm. Worth a
second pair of eyes from whoever handled the loan.

## One improvement made

The Naturalist quiz said only that Roosevelt published ornithological work "as a
teenager." The corpus gave the actual title, so it now names it: *The Summer Birds of
the Adirondacks in Franklin County, N.Y.*, 1877, when he was eighteen.

## Standing gap — closed

Hagedorn's *Roosevelt in the Bad Lands* (1921) is **now in the corpus** and the Badlands
quotations have been run down against it. See "RESOLVED" above. Three of the six
outstanding items were restored verbatim; three could not be and remain softened. The
one that matters most — the Library's founding line — is still not sourceable to
Roosevelt's own hand, and the Sioux Falls sentence of September 3, 1910 is the
recommended substitute for public copy.

One remaining gap, smaller: the Appendix stating Roosevelt's total cattle investment is
missing from our OCR of the book. That is the only place the ~$80,000 loss figure could
come from.

## The corpus contains one book twice, under two names (August 2026)

`Roger L. Di Silvestro - Theodore Roosevelt in the Badlands (2011).md` is **not
by Di Silvestro**. It is a clean transcription of **Hermann Hagedorn,
*Roosevelt in the Bad Lands* (1921)** — the same book as
`Hermann Hagedorn - Roosevelt in the Bad Lands (1921).md`, which is a poorer
OCR of the identical text. Confirmed: same Roosevelt Memorial Association
title page, same copyright line, same transcriber's note, and the string
"Di Silvestro" appears in the file zero times.

**Why this mattered.** A quotation found in both files looks like two
independent sources and is one. A whole batch of Badlands questions was built
believing Hagedorn had been corroborated by a 2011 author. Nineteen claims
across two quizzes were affected and have been re-attributed openly to
Hagedorn, most of them as recollections he gathered thirty-five years after
the events.

`tools/verify.py` now collapses the two files and reports the true book, so
this cannot recur silently. See `CORPUS_ALIASES` in that file.

**A gain, not only a loss.** The clean transcription contains the appendix the
OCR scan lost, which settles a question this document previously left open:

> Expended September 1884 – July 1885 $82,500.00
> Returns from cattle sales to December 1891 $42,443.32
> Stock on the range, December 1891 $16,500.00
> **Loss, not counting interest on the investment $23,556.68**

Later operations reduced the figure across both ventures to **$20,292.63** by
1899. Putnam gives the same numbers independently. So the "roughly $80,000
loss" this file recorded as unsourceable is resolved: $82,500 was the sum
*expended*, not the sum lost, and the two were being confused.
